## Sales
### S1 — Growth comes from more orders, not larger orders
- **Observation (fact):** Revenue rose in 2025 and 2026 while the average order value did not rise in step.
- **Evidence (calculated):** Revenue 29.5% (2025) and 20.4% (2026); orders 26.7% and 28.3%; AOV 2.2% and -6.2%. In 2024 revenue changed -2.8%. [Phase 4, A1]
- **Business interpretation:** The business is winning more transactions; basket size is flat to declining.
- **Potential implication:** Growth depends on order volume; a slowdown in order count would not be offset by larger orders.
- **Management question / possible action (recommendation):** Question: what drove the increase in orders (more customers, more frequent orders, or channel changes)? Track median order value next to AOV.
- **Limitation:** Synthetic years; AOV is pulled by a few large orders, so small AOV changes are not reliable signals.

### S2 — Revenue is concentrated in three months
- **Observation (fact):** September, November and December carry a large and stable share of each year's revenue.
- **Evidence (calculated):** Average share of annual revenue: 43.5% for Sep+Nov+Dec; 6.5% for Jan+Feb. [Phase 4, A2]
- **Business interpretation:** Month-on-month comparisons mostly reflect the calendar.
- **Potential implication:** Monthly performance reviews can mislead unless compared with the same month last year.
- **Management question / possible action (recommendation):** Action: report monthly KPIs against the same month last year and on a rolling 12-month basis (built into the dashboard).
- **Limitation:** Pattern observed over four synthetic years; causes of the seasonality are not in the data.

## Profitability
### P1 — Losses on individual lines give back a third of the profit earned elsewhere
- **Observation (fact):** Some order lines lose money; every one of them is a discounted line.
- **Evidence (calculated):** Loss-making lines lose 156.1k, equal to 35% of the profit on profitable lines; Binders, Tables, Machines account for 65% of losses. No full-price line loses money. [Phases 4-6]
- **Business interpretation:** Reported profit is the net of a profitable core and a concentrated loss-making tail.
- **Potential implication:** Reducing the tail would matter more to profit than growing the already-profitable core, provided volume held up (unknown).
- **Management question / possible action (recommendation):** Investigate the loss-making tail by sub-category, state and discount (dashboard page 3 and 5).
- **Limitation:** Profit allocation to lines in the source is not documented; profit appears mechanically generated (synthetic data).

### P2 — Furniture earns almost nothing, and not only because of discounting
- **Observation (fact):** Furniture has similar revenue and discount levels to the other categories but far lower profit.
- **Evidence (calculated):** Furniture: 32% of revenue, 6% of profit, margin 2.5% (Technology 17.4%, Office Supplies 17.0%); effective discount 20.0%, same as others. At the company discount mix its margin would be 4.8%. Full-price margins: Furniture 19%-27% vs median 36% elsewhere. [Phase 4, B1]
- **Business interpretation:** Furniture's list-price margins are thin, so ordinary discounts turn them into losses. Why list margins are thin is not visible in the data.
- **Potential implication:** Discount policy alone will not fix Furniture profitability.
- **Management question / possible action (recommendation):** Question: are Furniture list prices, purchase costs or freight the cause? Requires cost data.
- **Limitation:** No cost, freight or price-history data; the cause cannot be determined.

## Products
### PR1 — Almost half of revenue comes from products that lose money as a group
- **Observation (fact):** Products split into a profitable core and a large group of high-revenue, low-margin products.
- **Evidence (calculated):** Q1 core earners: 467 products, 44% of revenue, 92% of profit. Q2 margin leaks: 346 products, 43% of revenue, profit -27.2k; effective discount 24.7% vs 13.0% for Q1. High-margin niche products: 4% of revenue. [Phase 5]
- **Business interpretation:** Revenue is being earned without profit on a large part of the range; niche high-margin products are too small to offset it.
- **Potential implication:** Product-range decisions based on revenue rankings would protect loss-making products.
- **Management question / possible action (recommendation):** Review discount eligibility and pricing for Q2 products, starting with the largest losses.
- **Limitation:** Thresholds are analyst choices (tested: conclusion holds); many products have few sales; product IDs are reused (composite key used).

### PR2 — No product is unprofitable at full price
- **Observation (fact):** Loss-making products lose money only on their discounted sales.
- **Evidence (calculated):** 306 products lose 77.1k; 20 products account for half of it, mostly Machines, Tables. 208 are profitable on their own full-price sales; 98 were never sold at full price. [Phase 5]
- **Business interpretation:** Product losses are a discount problem, not a product-cost problem, in this data.
- **Potential implication:** Removing products is not indicated by the evidence; the discount applied to them is.
- **Management question / possible action (recommendation):** Prioritise discount-rule review over range rationalisation.
- **Limitation:** A product's full-price and discounted sales occur in different states, so the comparison is not a controlled test.

## Customers
### C1 — Profit depends heavily on a small group of customers
- **Observation (fact):** A minority of customers produces most of the profit.
- **Evidence (calculated):** Top 10% of customers: 58% of customer profit. Core accounts (high revenue, high margin): 27% of customers, 90% of profit. [Phase 7]
- **Business interpretation:** Customer concentration is a dependency, not evidence of risk.
- **Potential implication:** The loss of a small number of accounts would have a disproportionate effect on profit.
- **Management question / possible action (recommendation):** Question: how well are the top accounts covered and served? (Account management data needed.)
- **Limitation:** Four-year window only; no information on account relationships or contracts.

### C2 — Unprofitable customers are a transaction pattern, not a stable customer type
- **Observation (fact):** One customer in five is unprofitable, and customer profitability does not persist over time.
- **Evidence (calculated):** 155 customers (20%) lose 71.2k; all bought at least one line beyond break-even, 154 are profitable on their other lines. Margin 2023-2024 vs 2025-2026: rho = -0.00; 30% stay in the same segment (about 25% by chance). Customers get the same discount as anyone in the same state and sub-category. [Phase 7]
- **Business interpretation:** Customer losses follow the discount rules applied to their orders. The data shows no customer-specific deals.
- **Potential implication:** Acting on individual 'unprofitable customers' is not supported by the evidence.
- **Management question / possible action (recommendation):** Address the rules and approvals behind loss-making orders, not the customers.
- **Limitation:** The lack of persistence may be an artefact of synthetic data; retest on real data before any customer-level action.

## Geography
### G1 — Regional margin differences track discount levels
- **Observation (fact):** Central has the lowest margin and the highest discount; West the reverse.
- **Evidence (calculated):** Central margin 7.9%, effective discount 24.6%; at the company discount mix 11.3% (about 74% of its gap). West 14.9% -> 10.4% after adjustment (discount 15.6%). [Phase 4, B3]
- **Business interpretation:** Regional rankings reflect discount rules more than regional performance.
- **Potential implication:** Judging regional managers on raw margin would reward low-discount regions regardless of performance.
- **Management question / possible action (recommendation):** Use mix-adjusted margin in regional scorecards; confirm who sets regional discount rules.
- **Limitation:** Standardisation controls for discount band only; region and discount cannot be fully separated.

### G2 — Every loss-making state is a heavy-discount state
- **Observation (fact):** Ten states lose money; all apply deep discounts.
- **Evidence (calculated):** 10 states lose 98.2k; all have an effective discount of at least 29.7%. Across 28 states with 30+ orders, discount vs margin rho = -0.87. Texas: 3rd-largest by revenue, loss 25.7k, discount 39.1%. [Phase 4, E1]
- **Business interpretation:** Whether a state loses money is closely associated with its discount rules.
- **Potential implication:** State-level losses are concentrated and identifiable.
- **Management question / possible action (recommendation):** Review the discount rules in Texas, Ohio, Pennsylvania and Illinois first.
- **Limitation:** Correlation across states; state and discount are confounded by design.

## Discounts
### D1 — Discount is a fixed rule by state and sub-category
- **Observation (fact):** Every order line in the same state and sub-category carries the same discount, and the rules never changed.
- **Evidence (calculated):** 0 of 656 state x sub-category rules contain more than one discount level; 0 of 517 rules seen in several years changed. 11 states never sell at full price. [Phases 4, 6]
- **Business interpretation:** Discounting behaves like a central pricing policy, not deal-by-deal negotiation.
- **Potential implication:** The losses come from rules that management can change. But the effect of changing them cannot be estimated from this data.
- **Management question / possible action (recommendation):** Obtain the rule table and its rationale; this is the single most important document for the next step.
- **Limitation:** May be an artefact of the synthetic data; must be confirmed against the real pricing policy.

### D2 — Deep discount rules account for almost all losses
- **Observation (fact):** Profitability falls with each discount band; every rule above 40% loses money.
- **Evidence (calculated):** Margin: 29.5% at full price, 11.9% at 1-20%, -15.3% at 21-40%. 79 loss-making rules lose 145.7k (93% of all line losses); the 32 rules above 40% lose 99.6k. Discount given away: 566.6k, 2.0x profit. [Phases 5, 6]
- **Business interpretation:** A small number of rules is associated with most of the profit erosion.
- **Potential implication:** Reviewing a few dozen rules covers most of the loss.
- **Management question / possible action (recommendation):** Review the 32 rules above 40% first, then loss-making 21-40% rules (dashboard page 5 list).
- **Limitation:** Association; loss at stake is the current loss, not a forecast of profit recovered.

### D3 — There is no sign that discounts buy volume
- **Observation (fact):** Discounted business does not show larger baskets, a larger share of purchases, or faster growth.
- **Evidence (calculated):** Discount vs units per line rho = 0.00; vs state revenue growth rho = -0.08; vs share of a state's purchases rho = 0.06. Order growth: 1.80x at full price vs 1.27-1.77x in discounted bands. Arithmetic: a 20% discount on a product with the median list margin (33%) needs 2.5x the units to keep profit. [Phase 6]
- **Business interpretation:** Deep discounting is not visibly generating profitable growth.
- **Potential implication:** Revenue beyond break-even (20% of revenue, loss 156.1k) erodes profit on every unit; whether it protects volume is unknown.
- **Management question / possible action (recommendation):** Run a controlled pilot: lower the deepest rules in a few states, keep comparable states unchanged, measure orders, units and profit.
- **Limitation:** No comparison group exists in the data; absence of association is not proof of no effect.

## What this analysis cannot tell management
- Whether reducing discounts would raise or lower total profit (no variation in discount within rules or over time).
- Why Furniture margins are thin, or why rules were set as they are (no cost or policy data).
- Customer lifetime value, churn, or which customers are at risk (left-censored history, no relationship data).
- Real-world timing or market context (synthetic years, no competitor data).
